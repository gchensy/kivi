Model performance#

Measure how well a score that rises with the event probability separates events from non-events. roc_auc(), gini() and ks() summarize the ranking, roc_table() lists the ROC curve, roc_auc_interval() and delong_test() add DeLong’s standard errors, gains_table() breaks the ranking down by score band, and brier_score() compares probabilities with outcomes.

See Model performance for the definitions, examples and how to read a gains table.

kivi.metrics.roc_auc(score, target, *, weights=None)#

Compute the area under the ROC curve of a score.

The AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen event has a higher score than a randomly chosen non-event, with ties counted as one half.

The score comes first and the target second, as in kivi.woe.woe_table; scikit-learn’s metrics take the target first.

Parameters:
scorearray_like

Finite numeric scores that are higher for a higher event probability. Negate scorecard points, which are higher for a lower risk.

targetarray_like

Binary labels, with 1 indicating the event. Both classes must be present. If score and target are both pandas Series, they must have identical indices; otherwise they are matched by position.

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative weight of each observation, such as the inverse of its sampling rate, matched like target. Every count becomes a sum of weights, so integer weights give the same result as repeating rows.

Returns:
float

The AUC, from 0 to 1. A score unrelated to the target gives about 0.5, and a value below 0.5 suggests that the score runs the other way.

Raises:
ValueError

If the inputs are empty or not one-dimensional, their indices or lengths do not match, target has a label other than 0 and 1 or lacks one of them, or score is not real and numeric or has a missing or infinite value. Also if weights is not numeric, does not give each observation one finite, non-negative weight, has an index other than that of score, or leaves a class without weight.

See also

gini

The same ranking measure on a scale from -1 to 1.

ks

Largest gap between the score distributions of the two classes.

gains_table

Event rates and captured events by score band.

Notes

With \(n_1\) events and \(n_0\) non-events, let \(R_1\) be the sum of the ranks of the events among all scores, where tied scores share their average rank. The Mann-Whitney statistic gives

\[\mathrm{AUC} = \frac{R_1 - n_1 (n_1 + 1) / 2}{n_1 n_0}.\]

With weights, each pair of an event \(i\) and a non-event \(j\) counts with weight \(w_i w_j\), and \(n_1\) and \(n_0\) are the total weights of the two classes.

References

[1]

J. A. Hanley and B. J. McNeil, “The meaning and use of the area under a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve”, Radiology, 143(1), pp. 29-36, 1982.

Examples

>>> from kivi.metrics import roc_auc
>>> score = [0.1, 0.4, 0.4, 0.8, 0.7, 0.9]
>>> target = [0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1]
>>> round(roc_auc(score, target), 4)
0.7222

Scorecard points are higher for a lower risk, so negate them:

>>> import numpy as np
>>> points = np.array([720, 650, 650, 560, 590, 510])
>>> round(roc_auc(-points, target), 4)
0.7222
kivi.metrics.gini(score, target, *, weights=None)#

Compute the Gini coefficient of a score.

The Gini coefficient rescales the AUC to run from -1 to 1, so that a score unrelated to the target gives about 0.

The score comes first and the target second, as in kivi.woe.woe_table; scikit-learn’s metrics take the target first.

Parameters:
scorearray_like

Finite numeric scores that are higher for a higher event probability. Negate scorecard points, which are higher for a lower risk.

targetarray_like

Binary labels, with 1 indicating the event. Both classes must be present. If score and target are both pandas Series, they must have identical indices; otherwise they are matched by position.

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative weight of each observation, such as the inverse of its sampling rate, matched like target. Every count becomes a sum of weights, so integer weights give the same result as repeating rows.

Returns:
float

The Gini coefficient, from -1 to 1. A negative value suggests that the score runs the other way.

Raises:
ValueError

If the inputs are empty or not one-dimensional, their indices or lengths do not match, target has a label other than 0 and 1 or lacks one of them, or score is not real and numeric or has a missing or infinite value. Also if weights is not numeric, does not give each observation one finite, non-negative weight, has an index other than that of score, or leaves a class without weight.

See also

roc_auc

The area under the ROC curve.

ks

Largest gap between the score distributions of the two classes.

Notes

The Gini coefficient, also called the accuracy ratio, is

\[\mathrm{Gini} = 2\,\mathrm{AUC} - 1.\]

Examples

>>> from kivi.metrics import gini
>>> score = [0.1, 0.4, 0.4, 0.8, 0.7, 0.9]
>>> target = [0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1]
>>> round(gini(score, target), 4)
0.4444
kivi.metrics.ks(score, target, *, weights=None)#

Compute the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic of a score.

The KS statistic is the largest gap between the cumulative distribution of the scores of events and that of non-events.

The score comes first and the target second, as in kivi.woe.woe_table; scikit-learn’s metrics take the target first.

Parameters:
scorearray_like

Finite numeric scores, such as predicted event probabilities.

targetarray_like

Binary labels, with 1 indicating the event. Both classes must be present. If score and target are both pandas Series, they must have identical indices; otherwise they are matched by position.

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative weight of each observation, such as the inverse of its sampling rate, matched like target. Every count becomes a sum of weights, so integer weights give the same result as repeating rows.

Returns:
float

The largest gap, from 0 to 1.

Raises:
ValueError

If the inputs are empty or not one-dimensional, their indices or lengths do not match, target has a label other than 0 and 1 or lacks one of them, or score is not real and numeric or has a missing or infinite value. Also if weights is not numeric, does not give each observation one finite, non-negative weight, has an index other than that of score, or leaves a class without weight.

See also

gains_table

The gap at the edge of each score band.

roc_auc

The probability that an event scores higher than a non-event.

Notes

Let \(F_1\) and \(F_0\) be the empirical distribution functions of the scores of events and of non-events. Then

\[\mathrm{KS} = \max_t \, \lvert F_1(t) - F_0(t) \rvert,\]

where \(t\) runs over the observed scores. With weights, the distribution functions are weighted. As in the two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, the gap is taken in absolute value, so negated scores give the same KS; use the sign of gini to check the direction.

The largest gap can come from a region where the score runs the wrong way, for example when events gather among the lowest scores as well as the highest. The KS then measures how far apart the two distributions are rather than how well the score ranks events above non-events. The cumulative shares in gains_table show where the gap occurs.

References

[1]

L. C. Thomas, D. B. Edelman and J. N. Crook, “Credit Scoring and Its Applications”, SIAM, 2002.

Examples

>>> from kivi.metrics import ks
>>> score = [0.1, 0.4, 0.4, 0.8, 0.7, 0.9]
>>> target = [0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1]
>>> round(ks(score, target), 4)
0.3333
kivi.metrics.roc_table(score, target, *, weights=None)#

Tabulate the ROC curve of a score, with one row per distinct score.

Each row flags the observations that score at or above its threshold and counts the events and non-events among them. Plotting true_positive_rate against false_positive_rate gives the ROC curve, from the highest threshold to the lowest.

The score comes first and the target second, as in kivi.woe.woe_table; scikit-learn’s metrics take the target first.

Parameters:
scorearray_like

Finite numeric scores that are higher for a higher event probability. Negate scorecard points, which are higher for a lower risk.

targetarray_like

Binary labels, with 1 indicating the event. Both classes must be present. If score and target are both pandas Series, they must have identical indices; otherwise they are matched by position.

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative weight of each observation, such as the inverse of its sampling rate, matched like target. The counts become sums of weights, and a score whose observations all have zero weight has no row, so integer weights give the same table as repeating rows.

Returns:
pandas.DataFrame

One row per threshold, in an index named threshold: first infinity, which flags no observation, then every distinct score from the highest to the lowest, which flags them all. The columns are described in the Notes.

Raises:
ValueError

If the inputs are empty or not one-dimensional, their indices or lengths do not match, target has a label other than 0 and 1 or lacks one of them, or score is not real and numeric or has a missing or infinite value. Also if weights is not numeric, does not give each observation one finite, non-negative weight, has an index other than that of score, or leaves a class without weight.

See also

roc_auc

The area under the curve.

ks

The largest gap between the two rates.

gains_table

The same cumulative shares by score band.

Notes

The columns are:

  • cum_non_event and cum_event: non-events and events that score at or above the threshold, or the sums of their weights;

  • false_positive_rate and true_positive_rate: these counts as shares of all non-events and of all events;

  • precision: cum_event divided by the sum of both counts, the event rate among the flagged observations, which is NaN in the first row.

Tied scores share a row, so the curve crosses a tie diagonally. The trapezoids under the curve therefore add up to roc_auc, which counts a tied pair as one half,

\[\mathrm{AUC} = \sum_{k \ge 1} (\mathrm{FPR}_k - \mathrm{FPR}_{k-1}) \, \frac{\mathrm{TPR}_k + \mathrm{TPR}_{k-1}}{2},\]

and the largest gap between the two rates, \(\max_k \lvert \mathrm{TPR}_k - \mathrm{FPR}_k \rvert\), is ks. The rows are those of sklearn.metrics.roc_curve with drop_intermediate=False.

Examples

>>> from kivi.metrics import roc_table
>>> score = [0.1, 0.4, 0.4, 0.8, 0.7, 0.9]
>>> target = [0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1]
>>> table = roc_table(score, target)
>>> table[["cum_non_event", "cum_event", "false_positive_rate", "true_positive_rate"]].round(3)
           cum_non_event  cum_event  false_positive_rate  true_positive_rate
threshold
inf                    0          0                0.000               0.000
0.9                    0          1                0.000               0.333
0.8                    1          1                0.333               0.333
0.7                    1          2                0.333               0.667
0.4                    2          3                0.667               1.000
0.1                    3          3                1.000               1.000
>>> table["precision"].round(3).tolist()
[nan, 1.0, 0.5, 0.667, 0.6, 0.5]
kivi.metrics.roc_auc_interval(score, target, *, level=0.95, weights=None)#

Estimate the standard error and a confidence interval of the AUC.

The standard error is that of DeLong, DeLong and Clarke-Pearson [1], which makes no assumption about the distribution of the scores. The interval of the Gini coefficient follows from that of the AUC.

The score comes first and the target second, as in kivi.woe.woe_table; scikit-learn’s metrics take the target first.

Parameters:
scorearray_like

Finite numeric scores that are higher for a higher event probability. Negate scorecard points, which are higher for a lower risk.

targetarray_like

Binary labels, with 1 indicating the event. Without weights there must be at least two events and two non-events. If score and target are both pandas Series, they must have identical indices; otherwise they are matched by position.

levelfloat, default 0.95

Confidence level of the interval, between 0 and 1.

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative frequency weight of each observation, matched like target. An observation of weight \(w\) counts as \(w\) observations, so integer weights give the same result as repeating rows, standard error included. The weights of each class must add up to more than 1.

Returns:
pandas.DataFrame

Two rows, auc and gini, and the columns estimate, se for the standard error, and lower and upper for the bounds of the interval.

Raises:
ValueError

If the inputs are empty or not one-dimensional, their indices or lengths do not match, target has a label other than 0 and 1 or, without weights, fewer than two of either, or score is not real and numeric or has a missing or infinite value. Also if level is not a number between 0 and 1, or if weights is not numeric, does not give each observation one finite, non-negative weight, has an index other than that of score, or gives a class a total weight of 1 or less.

See also

roc_auc

The AUC alone.

delong_test

Compare the AUCs of two scores on the same observations.

Notes

Let \(n_1\) be the number of events and \(n_0\) that of non-events. The placement value \(V_{10,i}\) of event \(i\) is the share of non-events that score below it, and the placement value \(V_{01,j}\) of non-event \(j\) is the share of events that score above it, with ties counted as one half. Both average to the AUC \(\hat\theta\), and its variance is estimated by

\[\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\theta) = \frac{S_{10}}{n_1} + \frac{S_{01}}{n_0}, \qquad S_{10} = \frac{1}{n_1 - 1} \sum_{i=1}^{n_1} (V_{10,i} - \hat\theta)^2,\]

with \(S_{01}\) defined in the same way over the non-events. The placement values come from midranks, as in the algorithm of Sun and Xu [2]: the rank of an event among all scores minus its rank among the events is the number of non-events below it, a tie counting as one half. The computation thus takes \(O(N \log N)\) time for \(N\) observations instead of comparing every pair.

The interval is \(\hat\theta \pm z \, \mathrm{SE}\), cut off at 0 and 1, where \(z\) is the quantile of the standard normal distribution at \((1 + \mathrm{level}) / 2\). For the Gini coefficient, \(2\,\mathrm{AUC} - 1\), the estimate and the bounds are rescaled in the same way and the standard error doubles.

The normal approximation is poor when the AUC is close to 0 or 1 or a class has few observations. The standard error is 0 whenever the placement values are constant within each class, as they are for a score that separates the classes perfectly, one that reverses them and a constant score, so the interval collapses to the estimate.

With weights, \(n_1\) and \(n_0\) are the total weights of the two classes, and each squared deviation counts with the weight of its observation. Weights that stand for a population larger than the sample, such as inverse sampling rates, therefore give too small a standard error.

References

[1]

E. R. DeLong, D. M. DeLong and D. L. Clarke-Pearson, “Comparing the areas under two or more correlated receiver operating characteristic curves: a nonparametric approach”, Biometrics, 44(3), pp. 837-845, 1988.

[2]

X. Sun and W. Xu, “Fast implementation of DeLong’s algorithm for comparing the areas under correlated receiver operating characteristic curves”, IEEE Signal Processing Letters, 21(11), pp. 1389-1393, 2014.

Examples

Six observations leave a wide interval, whose upper bound is cut off at 1:

>>> from kivi.metrics import roc_auc_interval
>>> score = [0.1, 0.4, 0.4, 0.8, 0.7, 0.9]
>>> target = [0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1]
>>> roc_auc_interval(score, target).round(4)
      estimate      se   lower  upper
auc     0.7222  0.2485  0.2353    1.0
gini    0.4444  0.4969 -0.5295    1.0
kivi.metrics.delong_test(current, new, target, *, level=0.95, weights=None)#

Test whether two scores of the same observations have the same AUC.

DeLong’s test [1] compares two scores of the same sample, such as a new model and the model it would replace. Both scores rank the same pairs of events and non-events, so their AUCs are correlated, and the standard error of the difference takes that into account.

The scores come first and the target last, as in kivi.strategy.swap_set; scikit-learn’s metrics take the target first.

Parameters:
currentarray_like

Finite numeric scores of one model, such as the model in use, that are higher for a higher event probability.

newarray_like

Finite numeric scores of the other model for the same observations, in the same direction.

targetarray_like

Binary labels, with 1 indicating the event. Without weights there must be at least two events and two non-events. Any two of current, new and target that are pandas Series must have identical indices; otherwise they are matched by position.

levelfloat, default 0.95

Confidence level of the interval of the difference, between 0 and 1.

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative frequency weight of each observation, matched like target. An observation of weight \(w\) counts as \(w\) observations, so integer weights give the same result as repeating rows. The weights of each class must add up to more than 1.

Returns:
pandas.Series

current_auc and new_auc, the AUCs of the two scores; difference, which is new_auc minus current_auc; se, its standard error; lower and upper, the bounds of its interval at level, cut off at -1 and 1; z, the difference divided by its standard error; and p_value, the two-sided p-value of z.

Raises:
ValueError

If the inputs are empty or not one-dimensional, their indices or lengths do not match, target has a label other than 0 and 1 or, without weights, fewer than two of either, or current or new is not real and numeric or has a missing or infinite value. Also if level is not a number between 0 and 1, or if weights is not numeric, does not give each observation one finite, non-negative weight, has an index other than that of a Series among the scores, or gives a class a total weight of 1 or less.

See also

roc_auc_interval

The standard error and interval of one AUC.

kivi.strategy.swap_set

The applicants that two scores decide differently at their cut-offs.

Notes

With the placement values of roc_auc_interval computed for each score, let \(D_{10,i}\) be the placement value of event \(i\) under the new score minus that under the current one, and \(D_{01,j}\) the same difference for non-event \(j\). They average to the difference of the AUCs, and its variance is estimated by

\[\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{new}} - \hat\theta_{\mathrm{current}}) = \frac{S^D_{10}}{n_1} + \frac{S^D_{01}}{n_0},\]

where \(S^D_{10}\) and \(S^D_{01}\) are the sample variances of \(D_{10,i}\) over the \(n_1\) events and of \(D_{01,j}\) over the \(n_0\) non-events. This equals the variance of the contrast in DeLong et al., which adds the two variances and subtracts twice the covariance. Under the hypothesis of equal AUCs, \(z\) is approximately standard normal.

For a one-sided test that the new score ranks better, halve p_value when z is positive. When the two scores order every pair of an event and a non-event in the same way, the difference and its standard error are both 0, and z and p_value are NaN; with fractional weights, a difference and a standard error that are both within rounding error of 0 count as 0. A difference with a standard error of 0, such as that between a constant score and one that separates the classes, gives an infinite z and a p_value of 0.

Weights count as in roc_auc_interval, so inverse sampling rates give too small a standard error.

References

[1]

E. R. DeLong, D. M. DeLong and D. L. Clarke-Pearson, “Comparing the areas under two or more correlated receiver operating characteristic curves: a nonparametric approach”, Biometrics, 44(3), pp. 837-845, 1988.

Examples

The new score separates the six observations perfectly:

>>> from kivi.metrics import delong_test
>>> current = [0.1, 0.4, 0.4, 0.8, 0.7, 0.9]
>>> new = [0.2, 0.3, 0.6, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9]
>>> target = [0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1]
>>> delong_test(current, new, target).round(4)
current_auc    0.7222
new_auc        1.0000
difference     0.2778
se             0.2485
lower         -0.2092
upper          0.7647
z              1.1180
p_value        0.2636
dtype: float64
kivi.metrics.gains_table(score, target, *, bins=10, weights=None)#

Summarize events by score band, from the highest scores to the lowest.

Each row shows how many events a band holds and what share of all events is captured by reviewing that band and every band above it.

The score comes first and the target second, as in kivi.woe.woe_table; scikit-learn’s metrics take the target first.

Parameters:
scorearray_like

Finite numeric scores that are higher for a higher event probability. Negate scorecard points, which are higher for a lower risk.

targetarray_like

Binary labels, with 1 indicating the event. Both classes must be present. If score and target are both pandas Series, they must have identical indices; otherwise they are matched by position.

binsint or array_like, default 10

Number of quantile bands, or increasing band edges. An integer is the same as passing kivi.binning.bin_edges(score, bins=bins), so tied scores can leave fewer bands than requested. Pass the edges fitted on a development sample to compare later samples on the same bands. With weights, an integer gives weighted quantile edges, as bin_edges(score, bins=bins, weights=weights) does.

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative weight of each observation, such as the inverse of its sampling rate, matched like target. The counts become sums of weights. On the same edges, integer weights give the same table as repeating rows; an integer bins can give other edges, because weighted quantiles use the inverted CDF.

Returns:
pandas.DataFrame

One row per score band, from the highest band to the lowest, indexed by the interval of the band. The columns are described in the Notes.

Raises:
ValueError

If the inputs are empty or not one-dimensional, their indices or lengths do not match, target has a label other than 0 and 1 or lacks one of them, score is not real and numeric or has a missing or infinite value, bins is neither a positive integer nor increasing edges, or a score lies outside the edges. Also if weights is not numeric, does not give each observation one finite, non-negative weight, has an index other than that of score, or leaves a class without weight.

Warns:
UserWarning

If bins is an integer and tied scores leave fewer bands than requested, as in kivi.binning.bin_edges.

See also

ks

The largest gap over all scores rather than band edges.

roc_auc

Ranking quality over all pairs of events and non-events.

kivi.binning.bin_edges

Fit band edges on a development sample.

Notes

Bands are intervals closed on the right, as in pandas.cut, so tied scores always share a band. Every band is reported, including bands without observations, so samples cut on the same edges give tables with the same rows. An empty band has a NaN event_rate and lift, and cum_lift is NaN until the first band with observations.

The columns are:

  • non_event, event and total: non-events, events and all observations in the band, or the sums of their weights;

  • event_rate: event divided by total;

  • lift: event_rate divided by the event rate of the sample;

  • cum_non_event_share, cum_event_share and cum_total_share: shares of all non-events, events and observations in the band and the bands above it;

  • ks: the absolute difference between cum_event_share and cum_non_event_share;

  • cum_lift: cum_event_share divided by cum_total_share.

The largest ks is at most the value of ks, and the two are equal when each distinct score forms its own band.

Examples

>>> from kivi.metrics import gains_table
>>> score = [1, 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 1, 2]
>>> target = [0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0]
>>> table = gains_table(score, target, bins=3)
>>> table[["total", "event", "event_rate", "cum_event_share", "ks", "lift"]].round(3)
            total  event  event_rate  cum_event_share     ks  lift
bin
(3.0, inf]      2      2         1.0            0.667  0.667  2.667
(2.0, 3.0]      2      1         0.5            1.000  0.800  1.333
(-inf, 2.0]     4      0         0.0            1.000  0.000  0.000
kivi.metrics.brier_score(probability, target, *, weights=None)#

Compute the Brier score of predicted event probabilities.

The Brier score is the mean squared difference between the predicted probability and the observed outcome, so a lower value is better.

The probabilities come first and the target second, as in kivi.woe.woe_table; scikit-learn’s metrics take the target first.

Parameters:
probabilityarray_like

Predicted event probabilities from 0 to 1.

targetarray_like

Binary labels, with 1 indicating the event. If probability and target are both pandas Series, they must have identical indices; otherwise they are matched by position.

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative weight of each observation, matched like target. The score is then the weighted mean of the squared differences.

Returns:
float

The Brier score, from 0 for perfect forecasts to 1.

Raises:
ValueError

If the inputs are empty or not one-dimensional, their indices or lengths do not match, target has a label other than 0 and 1, or probability is not real and numeric or has a missing value or a value outside 0 to 1. Also if weights is not numeric, does not give each observation one finite, non-negative weight with a positive total, or has an index other than that of probability.

See also

roc_auc

Ranking quality, which ignores the probability scale.

Notes

For outcomes \(y_i\) and probabilities \(p_i\),

\[\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (p_i - y_i)^2,\]

or \(\sum_i w_i (p_i - y_i)^2 / \sum_i w_i\) with weights.

Unlike the ranking measures, the Brier score depends on the probability values themselves, so it rewards calibration, and it is defined even when the sample has only one class. Predicting the event rate \(\bar y\) for everyone scores \(\bar y (1 - \bar y)\), a useful baseline.

References

[1]

G. W. Brier, “Verification of forecasts expressed in terms of probability”, Monthly Weather Review, 78(1), pp. 1-3, 1950.

Examples

>>> from kivi.metrics import brier_score
>>> target = [0, 0, 1, 1]
>>> round(brier_score([0.1, 0.3, 0.6, 0.8], target), 4)
0.075
