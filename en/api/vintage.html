Vintage and roll-rate analysis#

Analyse a loan panel as the loans age. delinquency_panel() builds the panel from instalment schedules and payments, vintage_table() gives the cumulative bad rate of each cohort by month on book, default_rate_table() the one-year default rate of each grade from a series of start months, and transition_matrix() and roll_rates() show how loans move between delinquency buckets or grades. migration_bandwidth() and migration_test() summarize and test the migrations between rating grades.

See Vintage and roll-rate analysis for examples on a synthetic loan panel, on a few repayment schedules and on simulated rating migrations.

kivi.vintage.delinquency_panel(loan, due_date, amount_due, payment_loan, payment_date, payment_amount, *, end, origination=None, principal=None, tolerance=0.0)#

Build a monthly delinquency panel from instalment schedules and payments.

At every month end, a loan is behind by the instalments that have fallen due and are not paid yet. This function applies the payments of each loan to its instalments, the oldest first, and gives one row per loan and month end with the days past due, the delinquency status and the amounts past due and outstanding: the loan panel that vintage_table, transition_matrix and roll_rates take.

Parameters:
loanarray_like

Loan of each instalment of the schedule. The instalments need not be sorted.

due_datearray_like

Due date of each instalment, as dates or as strings that pandas.to_datetime reads. A time of day is ignored.

amount_duearray_like

Amount of each instalment, finite and non-negative, such as the principal and the interest due on that date.

payment_loanarray_like

Loan of each payment. Every one must be a loan of the schedule, and a loan without payments has paid nothing. The payments need not be sorted, and their Series may have any index.

payment_datearray_like

Date of each payment, read like due_date.

payment_amountarray_like

Amount of each payment, finite and non-negative. Net a reversal against the payment that it reverses.

enddate or month

Last month of the panel, such as "2026-01", a date in that month or a pandas.Period. A string is read as pandas.Period reads it, and a period longer than a month, such as "2025Q4", ends in its last month. The payments must be complete up to its last day.

originationpandas.Series or mapping, optional

Origination date of each loan, as a Series indexed by loan or a mapping from loan to date, read like due_date. It must give a date for every loan of the schedule and may hold other loans too. The cohorts are then the origination months, and the months on book count from them. By default, both come from the first due dates.

principalarray_like, optional

Principal part of each instalment, between 0 and its amount due, matched to the schedule like amount_due. The panel then gets a balance column.

tolerancefloat, default 0.0

Largest unpaid amount of an instalment that still counts as paid, such as a few cents of rounding. It decides only which instalments count as paid; the amounts in the panel stay exact.

Returns:
pandas.DataFrame

One row per loan and month end, sorted by loan and then by month, with the columns described in the Notes: those of kivi.datasets.make_loan_panel that a schedule gives, in the same order, and the days past due and the amounts owed.

Raises:
ValueError

If the inputs of the schedule, or those of the payments, are not one-dimensional, have different lengths or are Series with different indices, or if the schedule is empty. Also if a loan or a date is missing, a date cannot be read, an amount is missing, negative, infinite or not a number, principal exceeds amount_due, a payment belongs to a loan that is not in the schedule, end is not a date or a month or comes before the month of every due date, origination is not a Series or a mapping, repeats a loan, lacks a loan of the schedule or its date, or comes after the first due date of its loan, or tolerance is not a finite, non-negative number.

See also

vintage_table

Cumulative bad rates by cohort and month on book.

transition_matrix

Movements between delinquency buckets.

kivi.datasets.make_loan_panel

Synthetic loan panel with the same statuses.

Notes

A loan has a row at the end of every month from the month of its first due date to end. It leaves the panel after the month end at which all its instalments count as paid, also when it is repaid early, while a loan that is still behind after its last due date keeps rolling until end. Payments after end are not used, and neither are amounts paid beyond the total due, such as an overpayment or a payment after the loan was repaid: there is no credit balance.

At each month end \(t\), only the payments made on or before \(t\) count. They go to the instalments in the order of their due dates, the oldest unpaid one first and instalments due on the same day in the order of the schedule, so an amount paid before an instalment falls due counts towards it. With \(C_k\) the total amount due by instalment \(k\) of a loan in that order, and \(P_t\) the total paid by \(t\), instalment \(k\) counts as paid at \(t\) if

\[C_k - P_t \le \mathrm{tolerance},\]

that is, once at most tolerance remains unpaid of it and the instalments before it together. A partial payment leaves an instalment unpaid until then, and an unpaid remainder within tolerance is still owed: the next payments go to it first. A loan therefore leaves the panel once at most tolerance of it is unpaid, so a tolerance of at least its total due makes a loan that pays nothing leave in its first month. Amounts within a relative \(10^{-10}\) of \(C_k\) count as paid, so that the rounding of sums does not leave unpaid an instalment paid in parts. Instalments due on the same day add up, so a schedule row that a faulty join repeats doubles the amount due.

If \(d\) is the due date of the oldest instalment that is not paid at \(t\), the loan is \(t - d\) days past due when \(d\) is before \(t\), and 0 days otherwise; an instalment is not past due on its due date.

The columns are:

  • loan_id: the loan, with the labels of loan;

  • cohort: the origination month of the loan with origination, and otherwise the month before its first due date, a monthly pandas.Period;

  • month: the month, a monthly pandas.Period, observed on its last day;

  • month_on_book: month - cohort in months. Without origination, it is 1 in the month of the first due date, as in kivi.datasets.make_loan_panel, whose first instalment falls due one month after origination. With origination, a loan whose first instalment falls due later, after a grace period, has its first row at a later month on book;

  • days_past_due: the days past due, as defined above;

  • overdue_amount: the unpaid amount of the instalments due before the month end;

  • outstanding: the unpaid amount of all instalments, past due or not;

  • balance, only with principal: the unpaid principal of all instalments. A payment pays the interest of an instalment, its amount due less its principal, before its principal;

  • status: the days past due as an ordered categorical with the categories current (0 days), 1-29, 30-59, 60-89 and 90+ (90 days or more), the statuses of kivi.datasets.make_loan_panel.

Without origination, loans originated in the same month but with grace periods of different lengths fall into different cohorts. With it, a cohort whose loans all have a grace period starts at a later month on book in vintage_table, which leaves the earlier ones NaN.

Days past due at month ends do not always move one bucket a month. An instalment due on 31 January and still unpaid is 29 days past due at the end of February and 60 days at the end of March, so it skips 30-59.

Examples

>>> from kivi.vintage import delinquency_panel
>>> loan = ["A"] * 3 + ["B"] * 3
>>> due_date = ["2024-01-15", "2024-02-15", "2024-03-15"] * 2
>>> amount_due = [100.0] * 6
>>> payment_loan = ["A", "A", "B"]
>>> payment_date = ["2024-01-15", "2024-03-20", "2024-01-12"]
>>> payment_amount = [100.0, 200.0, 200.0]
>>> panel = delinquency_panel(
...     loan, due_date, amount_due, payment_loan, payment_date, payment_amount, end="2024-05"
... )
>>> panel.drop(columns=["cohort", "month_on_book"])
  loan_id    month  days_past_due  overdue_amount  outstanding   status
0       A  2024-01              0             0.0        200.0  current
1       A  2024-02             14           100.0        200.0     1-29
2       A  2024-03              0             0.0          0.0  current
3       B  2024-01              0             0.0        100.0  current
4       B  2024-02              0             0.0        100.0  current
5       B  2024-03             16           100.0        100.0     1-29
6       B  2024-04             46           100.0        100.0    30-59
7       B  2024-05             77           100.0        100.0    60-89

Loan A missed its February instalment and paid it with the March one on 20 March, after the end of February, so it was 14 days past due at the end of February and is repaid at the end of March. Loan B paid two instalments in January, which keeps it current in February, and has not paid the last one, so it keeps rolling after its last due date.

kivi.vintage.vintage_table(loan, cohort, month_on_book, bad, *, weights=None)#

Tabulate the cumulative bad rate of each cohort by month on book.

A vintage table follows each cohort of loans, usually the loans originated in the same month, as it ages. Its entry for a cohort and a month on book is the share of the cohort’s loans that were bad at or before that month on book, so every row is non-decreasing.

Parameters:
loanarray_like

Identifier of the loan of each row of the panel. A loan has at most one row per month on book, and its rows need not be sorted.

cohortarray_like

Cohort of each row, such as the month of origination. It must be the same on every row of a loan. Cohorts are sorted, and categorical cohorts follow the order of their categories.

month_on_bookarray_like

Whole, non-negative number of months since origination of each row.

badarray_like

Whether the loan is bad at that month on book, as booleans or 0 and 1. Pass the bad definition as a condition on the status, such as status == "90+", or status >= "60-89" for an ordered categorical.

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative weight of each loan, such as the amount lent, repeated on every row of the loan. The table then reports the share of the cohort’s weight in loans that were bad, and cohorts whose weights sum to zero are left out.

Returns:
pandas.DataFrame

Cumulative bad rates with one row per cohort and one column per distinct month on book of the panel. An entry is NaN outside the months on book at which the cohort is observed, from the smallest to the largest month on book of its rows.

Raises:
ValueError

If the inputs are empty, not one-dimensional or of different lengths, or Series with different indices. Also if loan or cohort has a missing value, a loan has more than one cohort or more than one row for a month on book, month_on_book has a value that is not a whole, non-negative number, bad has a value other than 0 and 1, or weights is not valid or differs between the rows of a loan.

See also

transition_matrix

Movements between states from one month to another.

roll_rates

Share of each delinquency bucket that rolls to the next.

kivi.datasets.make_loan_panel

Synthetic loan panel to try this on.

Notes

Let \(L_c\) be the loans of cohort \(c\), \(\tau_i\) the first month on book at which loan \(i\) is bad (infinite if it never is) and \(w_i\) its weight, 1 without weights. The entry for month on book \(k\) is

\[V_{c,k} = \frac{\sum_{i \in L_c} w_i \, [\tau_i \le k]} {\sum_{i \in L_c} w_i}.\]

The denominator includes every loan of the cohort, also loans that left the panel early, for example because they were repaid; such a loan counts as bad only if it was bad before it left. A loan that is bad once stays counted as bad, even if it later cures.

A cohort is observed from the smallest to the largest month on book of its rows, and its entries outside that range are NaN, not 0. Later months on book are missing because the cohort is too young to show whether its loans will go bad, and earlier ones because the panel starts after the cohort was originated. Loans that were bad only before the panel starts are not counted, so start the panel at origination where possible. Comparing the cohorts at the same month on book compares them at the same age, which separates a change in the quality of new lending from the seasoning of the portfolio.

Two cases need care. If every loan of a cohort leaves the panel before it ends, for example because all of them were repaid, the cohort’s last entry is final, yet its later months on book are NaN; to fill them, keep a row for each month end after a loan left, with a status that is not bad, such as "closed". And a cohort that spans several origination months, such as a quarter, mixes loans of different ages: at its largest months on book, its youngest loans count in the denominator although they have not been observed that long, which biases those entries down. Monthly cohorts avoid this.

Examples

>>> from kivi.vintage import vintage_table
>>> loan = [1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4]
>>> cohort = ["2024-01"] * 6 + ["2024-02"] * 4
>>> month_on_book = [1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 1, 2]
>>> status = ["current", "90+", "current", "current", "current", "current",
...           "90+", "90+", "current", "current"]
>>> bad = [value == "90+" for value in status]
>>> vintage_table(loan, cohort, month_on_book, bad)
month_on_book    1    2    3
cohort
2024-01        0.0  0.5  0.5
2024-02        0.5  0.5  NaN

Loan 1 cured after month on book 2 but stays counted as bad, and the 2024-02 cohort has not reached month on book 3.

kivi.vintage.default_rate_table(loan, month, grade, defaulted, *, horizon=12, starts=None, probability=None, weights=None)#

Tabulate the one-year default rate of each grade by the cohort approach.

Each start month forms a cohort of the loans that are not in default at its end, grouped by their grade at that month. The default rate of a grade is the share of its cohort that is in default in at least one month of the horizon months that follow, as the one-year default rate of IRB PD calibration and validation counts it. Loans that leave the panel during those months stay in the cohort.

Parameters:
loanarray_like

Identifier of the loan of each row of a monthly panel, or of the obligor where the definition of default applies to obligors. A loan has at most one row per month, and its rows need not be sorted.

montharray_like

Month of each row, for the status at its end: monthly periods, or datetimes, which stand for their month on their own calendar, such as the month of delinquency_panel.

gradearray_like

Grade or pool of each row. Only the row of a start month counts, so later migrations do not move a loan to another grade. Categorical grades follow the order of their categories and other grades are sorted. A missing grade forms a row of its own, listed last.

defaultedarray_like

Whether the loan is in default in that month, as booleans or 0 and 1, such as status == "90+".

horizonint, default 12

Number of months that follow each start month, 12 for the one-year default rate.

startsarray_like, optional

Start months of the cohorts, as periods, dates or strings, which are read as a pandas.Period and stand for its last month, so "2024Q2" is June 2024. The horizon months after each one must lie in the panel. By default, every quarter end from the first month of the panel whose horizon months end at the last month of the panel or before.

probabilityarray_like, optional

PD of each row, between 0 and 1. The table then gives the mean PD of each cohort at its start month. Only the rows of the cohorts are checked for missing values, so defaulted rows may leave it empty.

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative frequency weight of each row, such as the inverse of a sampling rate; the weight of the start row of a loan counts. Groups whose weights sum to zero are left out. Do not weight by exposure for PD calibration, which counts obligors (Basel CRE36.78).

Returns:
pandas.DataFrame

One row per start month and grade with obligors in the cohort, with a MultiIndex of start, monthly periods, and grade, sorted by start month and then by grade. The columns are:

  • total: the loans of the cohort, or their weight;

  • event: those in default in at least one of the horizon months after the start month;

  • exited: those not in default in those months whose last row is before the last of them, such as loans repaid, sold or written off; they count in total;

  • event_rate: event / total, the default rate;

  • mean_probability: the mean PD of the cohort at the start month, only with probability.

The counts are integers without weights and sums of weights with them.

Raises:
ValueError

If the inputs are empty, not one-dimensional or of different lengths, or Series with different indices. Also if loan or month has a missing value, a loan has two rows in one month, month does not hold monthly periods or datetimes, defaulted has a value other than 0 and 1, horizon is not a positive integer, starts is not a list of distinct months whose horizon months lie in the panel, the panel covers no quarter end and the horizon months after it, or probability or weights is not valid.

See also

vintage_table

Cumulative bad rates by origination cohort and month on book.

transition_matrix

Movements between grades from one month to another.

kivi.calibration.jeffreys_test

Test the PD of each grade against its default rate.

kivi.calibration.central_tendency_shift

Shift PDs to a long-run average default rate.

kivi.capital.vasicek_fit

Long-run PD and asset correlation from yearly default rates.

Notes

Let \(C_{r,g}\) be the loans that have a row at start month \(r\), are not in default in it and have grade \(g\) there, and \(w_i\) the weight of the row of loan \(i\) at \(r\), 1 without weights. With \(h\) the horizon, the default rate is

\[\mathrm{DR}_{r,g} = \frac{\sum_{i \in C_{r,g}} w_i \, [\text{loan } i \text{ is in default in a month } m,\ r < m \le r + h]} {\sum_{i \in C_{r,g}} w_i}.\]

This is the one-year default rate of the EBA guidelines on PD estimation [1] (paragraphs 73 to 77) and of the ECB’s validation reporting [2] (section 2.3): the denominator holds every obligor not in default at the start, the numerator those with at least one default in the year, and an obligor that defaults twice counts once. Loans that migrate to another grade or model, are repaid, sold or written off during the year stay in the denominator, and their defaults count as long as the panel keeps their rows (paragraph 76). A loan without a row in some months of the window counts as not in default in them, so it is not censored as in a survival curve, which gives a higher rate when many loans leave: the exited column shows how many did, for the analysis of the bias that paragraphs 76 and 80 ask for. A missing grade is reported on its own row, as paragraph 75 asks for obligors without a rating at the start; the ECB’s reporting leaves them out (section 2.5.1).

The guidelines evaluate the one-year default rates at least quarterly (paragraph 78), which the default starts do, and leave the choice between overlapping windows, such as these, and non-overlapping ones, one start month a year, to an analysis of their bias (paragraph 80). The observed average default rate is the arithmetic mean of the one-year default rates (paragraph 81), and the long-run average is that mean over a period that is representative of the likely range of default rates, at least five years (paragraphs 82 to 86).

Month-end statuses miss a default that starts and ends within one month, so defaulted should flag a month in which the loan was in default at any time, including the probation period after it. Leave out technical defaults and other defaults recorded in error.

References

[1]

European Banking Authority, “Guidelines on PD estimation, LGD estimation and the treatment of defaulted exposures”, EBA/GL/2017/16, section 5.3, 2017.

[2]

European Central Bank, “Instructions for reporting the validation results of internal models: IRB Pillar I models for credit risk”, February 2019.

Examples

>>> import pandas as pd
>>> from kivi.vintage import default_rate_table
>>> months = [f"2024-{number:02d}" for number in range(1, 10)]
>>> rows = (
...     [(1, month, "A", month in ("2024-05", "2024-06")) for month in months]
...     + [(2, month, "A", False) for month in months[:4]]
...     + [(3, month, "B" if month < "2024-08" else "A", False) for month in months]
...     + [(4, month, "B", month >= "2024-08") for month in months]
...     + [(5, month, "A", False) for month in months[4:]]
... )
>>> panel = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["loan", "month", "grade", "defaulted"])
>>> panel["month"] = pd.PeriodIndex(panel["month"], freq="M")
>>> default_rate_table(
...     panel["loan"], panel["month"], panel["grade"], panel["defaulted"], horizon=3
... )
               total  event  exited  event_rate
start   grade
2024-03 A          2      1       1         0.5
        B          2      0       0         0.0
2024-06 A          1      0       0         0.0
        B          2      1       0         0.5

Loan 1 defaults in May and is not in the June cohort; loan 2 is repaid after April and counts as not in default; loan 3 stays in grade B although it moves to A in August; loan 5 enters the panel in May.

kivi.vintage.transition_matrix(before, after, *, states=None, normalize=False, weights=None)#

Count the movements of entities between two states.

Each entity, such as a loan or an obligor, is observed in one state before and one state after, for example its delinquency bucket at two consecutive month ends or its rating grade at two annual reviews. Row \(i\), column \(j\) counts the entities that moved from state \(i\) to state \(j\). With normalize, each row holds the shares of the states after, the migration matrix of a rating system.

Parameters:
before, afterarray_like

State of each entity at the first and at the second observation. If both are Series, they must have identical indices; otherwise they are matched by position. Neither may contain missing values: drop the entities that were not observed twice, or give the entities that left a state such as "closed".

statesarray_like, optional

All states in order, for delinquency buckets and rating grades from the best to the worst. States without entities still get a row and a column. By default, the categories of categorical inputs, which must then be the same for both, or else the sorted distinct values. Sorting puts labels such as "current" after "90+", so pass states or ordered categoricals for delinquency buckets. A state must not be missing.

normalizebool, default False

If True, divide each row by its total, so that it holds the shares of the state’s entities that moved to each state. A row without entities is then NaN.

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative weight of each entity, such as its balance at the first observation, matched like after. The counts are then sums of weights.

Returns:
pandas.DataFrame

One row per state before and one column per state after, in the order of states. Without weights and normalize, the entries are integer counts.

Raises:
ValueError

If the inputs are empty, not one-dimensional, of different lengths or Series with different indices, have a missing value or a value that is not one of states, or weights is not valid. Also if states is not one-dimensional or has duplicates or missing values, or is not given while before and after are categoricals with different categories, or if normalize is not a boolean.

See also

roll_rates

Share of each state that moved to the next worse state.

vintage_table

Cumulative bad rates by cohort and month on book.

Notes

With \(n_{ij}\) entities moving from state \(i\) to state \(j\), the normalized entry

\[p_{ij} = \frac{n_{ij}}{\sum_k n_{ik}}\]

estimates the probability of moving to \(j\) from \(i\). If the movements follow a time-homogeneous Markov chain, the \(h\)-th power of the normalized matrix gives the probabilities over \(h\) periods.

Examples

>>> from kivi.vintage import transition_matrix
>>> before = ["A", "A", "A", "B", "B", "C"]
>>> after = ["A", "A", "B", "B", "C", "C"]
>>> transition_matrix(before, after, states=["A", "B", "C", "D"])
after   A  B  C  D
before
A       2  1  0  0
B       0  1  1  0
C       0  0  1  0
D       0  0  0  0
>>> transition_matrix(before, after, normalize=True).round(3)
after       A      B    C
before
A       0.667  0.333  0.0
B       0.000  0.500  0.5
C       0.000  0.000  1.0
kivi.vintage.roll_rates(before, after, *, states=None, weights=None)#

Compute the share of each state’s entities that rolled to the next state.

Roll rates summarize the movements of delinquency between two month ends: the roll rate of 30-59 days past due is the share of the loans at 30 to 59 days that are at 60 to 89 days a month later. The states must be ordered from the best to the worst, so that the next state is the next worse one.

Parameters:
before, afterarray_like

State of each entity at the first and at the second observation, as in transition_matrix. Neither may contain missing values.

statesarray_like, optional

All states from the best to the worst. Required unless before and after are ordered categoricals with the same categories, whose order is then used. A sorted order would be wrong for most labels, for example "current" would come after "90+".

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative weight of each entity, such as its balance at the first observation, matched like after. The rates are then shares of weight, the balance roll rates.

Returns:
pandas.Series

Roll rate of every state except the worst, which has no next state, indexed by the state. A state without entities has a NaN rate.

Raises:
ValueError

In the same cases as transition_matrix, and if states is not given while before and after are not ordered categoricals.

See also

transition_matrix

The movements between all pairs of states.

vintage_table

Cumulative bad rates by cohort and month on book.

Notes

With \(n_{ij}\) entities moving from state \(i\) to state \(j\), the roll rate of state \(i\) is

\[r_i = \frac{n_{i,i+1}}{\sum_k n_{ik}}.\]

Entities that improved, stayed or moved more than one state worse count in the denominator only. A loan can fall at most one delinquency bucket further behind from one month end to the next, so the product of the roll rates estimates the share of current loans that reach the worst bucket after one month per roll, if the movements follow a Markov chain.

Examples

>>> from kivi.vintage import roll_rates
>>> states = ["current", "30", "60", "90+"]
>>> before = ["current"] * 4 + ["30"] * 3 + ["60"] * 2 + ["90+"]
>>> after = ["current", "current", "current", "30",
...          "current", "30", "60", "60", "90+", "90+"]
>>> roll_rates(before, after, states=states).round(3)
before
current    0.250
30         0.333
60         0.500
Name: roll_rate, dtype: float64
kivi.vintage.migration_bandwidth(before, after, *, grades, weights=None)#

Measure how far customers migrate between rating grades, upwards and downwards.

The matrix weighted bandwidth (MWB) of the European Central Bank [1] summarizes a migration matrix in two numbers: how far the customers who moved to worse grades moved, and how far those who moved to better grades moved, each relative to the furthest move from their grade in either direction. A stable rating system has small values.

Parameters:
beforearray_like

Grade of each customer at the beginning of the observation period. Every value must be one of grades.

afterarray_like

Status of each customer at the end of the period: a grade, or any other label, such as "default" or "closed", for a customer who left the grades. If before and after are both Series, they must have identical indices; otherwise they are matched by position. Neither may contain missing values.

gradesarray_like

The \(K\) grades of the rating scale for non-defaulted customers, from the best to the worst, at least two.

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative frequency weight of each customer, matched like after: integer weights give the same result as repeating rows.

Returns:
pandas.Series

upper, the MWB of the migrations to worse grades, above the diagonal of the migration matrix, and lower, that of the migrations to better grades, below it, in an index named statistic. Each is between 0 and 1, and NaN when no customer moved in its direction.

Raises:
ValueError

If the inputs are empty, not one-dimensional, of different lengths or Series with different indices, or have a missing value, if before has a value that is not one of grades, or if weights is not valid. Also if grades is not one-dimensional, has fewer than two grades, duplicates or missing values.

See also

migration_test

Test the monotonicity of the migration matrix.

transition_matrix

The migration matrix itself.

Notes

Number the grades from 1, the best, to \(K\). With \(N_i\) customers in grade \(i\) at the beginning and \(N_{ij}\) of them in grade \(j\) at the end, \(p_{ij} = N_{ij} / N_i\), and

\[\mathrm{MWB}_\mathrm{upper} = \frac{1}{M_u} \sum_{i=1}^{K-1} \sum_{j=i+1}^{K} |i - j| \, N_i \, p_{ij}, \qquad M_u = \sum_{i=1}^{K-1} \max(|i - K|, |i - 1|) \, N_i \sum_{j=i+1}^{K} p_{ij},\]

with \(\mathrm{MWB}_\mathrm{lower}\) and \(M_l\) defined in the same way over \(j < i\), for \(i\) from 2 to \(K\). \(N_i\) counts the customers who defaulted or left as well, but \(N_i \, p_{ij} = N_{ij}\) counts only those who reached a grade, so the customers who left change neither bandwidth. With weights, the counts are sums of weights.

References

[1]

European Central Bank, “Instructions for reporting the validation results of internal models: IRB Pillar I models for credit risk”, February 2019, section 2.5.5.1.

Examples

>>> from kivi.vintage import migration_bandwidth
>>> before = ["A", "A", "A", "B", "B", "B", "C", "C"]
>>> after = ["A", "B", "C", "A", "B", "default", "B", "C"]
>>> migration_bandwidth(before, after, grades=["A", "B", "C"]).round(4)
statistic
upper    0.7500
lower    0.6667
dtype: float64

Of grade A, one customer moved one grade down and one two grades, where each could have moved two, so the upper MWB is (1 + 2) / (2 + 2). The customer of grade B who defaulted counts in neither bandwidth.

kivi.vintage.migration_test(before, after, *, grades, weights=None)#

Test whether the migration frequencies fall away from the diagonal of the matrix.

In a stable rating system, few customers move far: the share of a grade that moves to another grade should fall the further that grade lies from it. The z tests of the European Central Bank [1] compare each off-diagonal cell of the migration matrix with its neighbour towards the diagonal. A small p-value means that more customers reached the cell than its neighbour, a sign that the portfolio or the rating system shifted.

Parameters:
beforearray_like

Grade of each customer at the beginning of the observation period. Every value must be one of grades.

afterarray_like

Status of each customer at the end of the period: a grade, or any other label, such as "default" or "closed", for a customer who left the grades. If before and after are both Series, they must have identical indices; otherwise they are matched by position. Neither may contain missing values.

gradesarray_like

The \(K\) grades of the rating scale for non-defaulted customers, from the best to the worst, at least two.

weightsarray_like, optional

Non-negative frequency weight of each customer, matched like after: integer weights give the same result as repeating rows.

Returns:
pandas.DataFrame

One row for each of the \(K (K - 1)\) off-diagonal cells, in the order of grades, indexed by the grades before and after. total is the number of customers in the grade before, those who left included; share the share of them in the grade after; statistic the z statistic; and p_value its one-sided p-value.

Raises:
ValueError

In the same cases as migration_bandwidth.

See also

migration_bandwidth

How far the customers migrate.

transition_matrix

The migration matrix itself.

Notes

With \(N_i\) customers in grade \(i\) at the beginning, those who defaulted or left included, and the shares \(p_{ij}\) of them in grade \(j\) at the end, each cell is compared with its neighbour \(n\) towards the diagonal, \(n = j + 1\) below the diagonal (\(j < i\)) and \(n = j - 1\) above it (\(j > i\)):

\[z_{ij} = \frac{p_{in} - p_{ij}} {\sqrt{\left[p_{ij} (1 - p_{ij}) + p_{in} (1 - p_{in}) + 2 p_{ij} p_{in}\right] / N_i}},\]

whose denominator is the standard deviation of \(p_{in} - p_{ij}\) when the customers of grade \(i\) migrate independently. Under the null hypothesis \(p_{ij} \le p_{in}\), the p-value is \(\Phi(z_{ij})\), so the cells that are fuller than their neighbour get small p-values. As the European Central Bank asks, the statistic and the p-value are NaN where they are not defined: for a grade without customers, and where both shares are 0 or one of them is 1. With weights, the counts are sums of weights, so weights that are not frequencies, such as exposures, do not give standard normal statistics.

References

[1]

European Central Bank, “Instructions for reporting the validation results of internal models: IRB Pillar I models for credit risk”, February 2019, section 2.5.5.2.

Examples

>>> from kivi.vintage import migration_test
>>> before = ["A", "A", "A", "B", "B", "B", "C", "C"]
>>> after = ["A", "B", "C", "A", "B", "default", "B", "C"]
>>> migration_test(before, after, grades=["A", "B", "C"]).round(4)
              total   share  statistic  p_value
before after
A      B          3  0.3333     0.0000   0.5000
       C          3  0.3333     0.0000   0.5000
B      A          3  0.3333     0.0000   0.5000
       C          3  0.0000     1.2247   0.8897
C      A          2  0.0000     1.4142   0.9214
       B          2  0.5000     0.0000   0.5000

The customer of grade B who defaulted counts in its total. No cell holds more customers than its neighbour towards the diagonal, so no p-value is small.
